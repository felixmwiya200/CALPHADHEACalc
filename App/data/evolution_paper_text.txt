A B S T R A C T
High entropy alloys (HEAs) attracted intensive study as they exhibit remarkable properties in potentially harsh 
service conditions. In numerous performance evaluation indicators, corrosion susceptibility is a major concern 
when selecting materials for practical applications because it causes serious problems with safety and significant 
economic loss. This review paper primarily analyses the evolution of corrosion characteristics of 3d transition 
metal HEAs with various chemical compositions across different aqueous solutions. Additionally, the effect of the 
chemistry of materials on the corrosion characteristics of 3d transition metal HEAs across different aqueous 
solutions is also discussed. The 3d transition metal HEA elements considered in the analysis mainly include Al, 
Cr, Ni, Ti, and Co. Additionally, the other elements, such as N, Sn and rare earth were also discussed. Further, the 
evolution of corrosion susceptibility under different preparation methods was elaborated, including the 
manufacturing process and heat treatment. Specifically, the existence of oxidation film and its correlation with 
the change of corrosion resistance in 3d transition metal HEAs was explored. Finally, the prospects in the field of 
3d transition metal HEAs corrosion research are put forward. The review article will provide a quick guide for 
researchers intending to work on the corrosion performance of 3d transition metal HEAs.
1. Introduction
The composition of traditional metal alloys is determined by the 
utilisation of a singular element as the primary constituent, with the 
subsequent incorporation of a limited number of additional elements to 
effect alterations in the microstructure and properties. The proportional 
weight of the primary component typically ranges from 50 to 90% 
[1–3]. However, the solubility of the primary component is limited. 
When the concentration of trace elements exceeds this solubility 
threshold, the formation of intermetallic compounds occurs, which can 
result in substantial alterations to the properties of the alloy [4–7]. As 
society progresses, the demands on materials have become more 
exacting, and the design of traditional alloys has gradually reached a 
point of stagnation. In order to overcome this challenge, numerous re­
searchers in the field of materials science have made extensive efforts 
[8–10].
In 1990s, the concept of high entropy alloys (HEAs) were proposed 
by Yeh et al. [11] and Cantor et al. [12], which were characterised by the 
presence of multiple principal elements and a high degree of mixing 
entropy. At present, a substantial number of HEAs, consisting of a va­
riety of components, have been meticulously developed. However, the 
precise definition of these novel alloys remains a subject of ongoing 
debate. Generally speaking, the definition of HEAs is generally divided 
into two categories: compositional definition and entropy range-based 
definition [13,14]. The definition of HEA is divided into two main ap­
proaches: composition-based and entropy range-based [11].
For the compositional definition, the HEAs typically contained five 
or more elements in equal or nearly equal atomic ratios, with each 
component constituting between 5 and 35% atomic percent [15]. 
Additionally, the HEAs can be divided into low, medium and high en­
tropy alloys according to the entropy values R [16], namely low entropy 
(SSSS,ideal < 0.69R), medium entropy (0.69R < SSSS,ideal < 1.61R) and 
low entropy (SSSS,ideal > 1.61R) alloys. It is emphasized that the above 
mentioned definitions of HEAs are contradictory in some specific cases.

1.1. Relationship between the four significant effects in HEAs and 
corrosion susceptibility
Due to this unique design concept, HEAs typically exhibit four sig­
nificant effects: the high entropy effect, the lattice distortion effect, the 
slow diffusion effect and the cocktail effect, as shown in Fig. 1 [17]. Lots 
of investigations had been demonstrated that the HEAs displayed 
outstanding corrosion resistance compared to traditional alloys [18]. 
This enhanced corrosion resistance is primarily attributed to the for­
mation of simple, disordered solid solutions with face-centered cubic 
(FCC) [19–21], body-centered cubic (BCC) [22–24], or close-packed 
hexagonal (HCP) structures [25–27], which decreases the possibility 
of elemental segregation. The relationship between the four main effects 
of HEAs and corrosion resistance can be summarized as follows.
1.1.1. The high entropy effect
The experimental findings demonstrate that HEAs composed of 
multiple compatible elements exhibit a propensity to form simple solid 
solutions rather than intermetallics, which is different from the results 
obtained on the basis of the classical Gibbs phase transition rule [15]. 
The presence of high entropy within an alloy is indicative of the exis­
tence of multiple elements, resulting in the formation of a complex solid 
solution [28]. This, in turn, leads to an increase in both the disorder and 
the stability of the alloy. The presence of a highly mixed elemental 
composition serves to render the surface of the alloy more resistant to 
the effects of corrosive media, due to the increased difficulty of the 
corrosion reaction engendered by the interaction between the different 
elements [29]. Moreover, the enhancement of corrosion resistance and 
passive properties of HEAs is concomitant with the uniform distribution 
of elements, a phenomenon attributable to the high-entropy effect of 
HEAs [30]. Then the process of galvanic corrosion is prevented in the 
presence of various phases of HEAs. It is imperative to note that this rule 
does not extend to all high entropy alloys.
1.1.2. The lattice distortion effect
The phenomenon of lattice distortion can be attributable to the 
disparate atomic dimensions of the constituent primary elements within 
HEA [31]. So far, no study has explored the relationship between lattice 
distortion effects and the corrosion resistance of HEAs. Fu et al. [15] 
pointed out that significant lattice distortion decreases the thermody­
namic stability of HEAs, potentially influencing the passivation behav­
iour of surface films on the alloys, and results in an altered mechanism 
for stress corrosion cracking (SCC). Furthermore, the relationship be­
tween lattice distortion and corrosive media in terms of the increase in 
energy of the system due to the increase in atomic spacing was illus­
trated by Varshney et al. [29,32]. Namely, the increase in the system’s 
potential energy can be associated with the variation in interatomic 
distance caused by the lattice distortion. Then the interaction between 
the HEA surface atoms and the corrosive environment changed.
1.1.3. The sluggish diffusion effect
As we know, the occurrence of SCC in metal alloys is contingent upon 
the interplay of numerous factors [33]. The hydrogen embrittlement 
failure mechanism, which serves as the important theory for elucidating 
SCC, posits that the hydrogen diffusion and its interaction with dislo­
cations play an important role in hydrogen embrittlement [33,34]. The 
sluggish diffusion effect of HEAs is closed related to the lattice disloca­
tion and the increase of mixing entropy. It has been demonstrated that 
lattice distortion has a significant effect on the resistance of dislocation 
motion [35]. Consequently, the mobility of dislocations in HEAs may not 
increase to the same extent as in traditional metal alloys under similar 
hydrogen concentrations. This suggests that localized deformation is less 
likely to occur in HEAs, which contributes to their higher resistance to 
hydrogen embrittlement.
1.1.4. The cocktail effect
The cocktail effect in HEAs refers to the synergistic interplay of 
multiple principal elements, which collectively contribute to properties 
that cannot be achieved by any single element or simple alloy [36]. This 
effect arises from the random and diverse mixture of elements in 
approximately equiatomic proportions, leading to unique mechanical, 
thermal, and chemical behaviors, including corrosion resistance [37]. 
The cocktail effect promotes the formation of oxide films enriched with 
protective elements like Cr [38], Al [39], Ti [40], and Mo [41]. These 
films are denser, more stable, and provide robust resistance to general 
and localized corrosion. The diverse alloying elements lead to passive

Due to the ‘cocktail’ effect, HEAs tend to form simple solid solution 
phases (e.g. FCC, BCC or HCP structures) rather than complex inter­

thermodynamic stability and chemical homogeneity, and exhibit supe­

‘cocktail’ effect may lead to a reduction in the energy of grain and phase 
boundaries, resulting in the formation of a fine, uniform grain structure 
[29]. This structure improves corrosion resistance because it is more 
difficult for corrosion processes to occur selectively in a homogeneous 
microstructure.
The unique composition, structure and its four significant effects (as 
shown in Fig. 1) give HEAs better comprehensive properties than 
traditional structural materials, making them a research focal point of 
potential structural materials. At present, lots of investigations have 
been conducted on the transformation of mechanical performances and 
microstructure of HEAs and the study on the corrosion susceptibility of 
HEAs is fragmented, with experimental results showing variance and, in 
some cases, even contradiction. Consequently, it is necessary to sys­
tematically review relevant literature on the corrosion mechanism of 
HEAs. This paper offers several recommendations for future studies on 
the corrosion susceptibility of HEAs, emphasizing the role of micro- 
alloying elements such as Al, Cr, Ni, Ti, and Co. It also highlights the 
importance of investigating processing methods and the stability of 
oxidation films in enhancing corrosion resistance.
2. Corrosion susceptibility evolution of HEAs with different 
chemical compositions
2.1. Al
Al is an essential element in the HEAs system, exerting a significant 
influence on the mechanical properties and corrosion behaviour of the 
material. The variation in corrosion resistance exhibited by HEAs in 
relation to their Al content is predominantly attributed to the volume of 
BCC and FCC phases [42–44]. Furthermore, other studies have demon­
strated that the addition of Al results in the formation of a passive film 
on HEAs in corrosive solutions [45–47].
The effect of Al content on the corrosion resistance of AlxCoCrFeNi 
HEAs in a 3.5 wt% NaCl solution are reported systematically by Shi et al. 
[48–52]. They verified that the change in corrosion resistance was 
linked to the microstructural evolution of AlxCoCrFeNi, resulting from 
variations in the Al content [50,51]. Typical single FCC phase was 
detected in the Al0.3CoCrFeNi alloy, consisting of a (Cr,Co,Fe)-rich 
structure with an even distribution of chemical elements. Then the BCC 
phase with the main element of Al and Ni appeared as the aluminum 
content increased in the Al0.5CoCrFeNi and Al0.7CoCrFeNi alloys. There 
was a notable increase in the volume fraction of the BCC phase, rising 
from 5% to 35%, as illustrated in Fig. 2 [51]. For the Al0.3CoCrFeNi 
HEAs, scattered pitting is observed after the polarization measurement. 
However, selective corrosion dissolution occurring at (Al, Ni)-rich zones 
is detected after polarization measurement and the FCC phase remains 
smooth for the Al0.5CoCrFeNi and Al0.7CoCrFeNi HEAs, as displayed in 
Fig. 3 [51]. Furthermore, the authors indicated that the observed fluc­
tuations in the AlxCoCrFeNi HEAs during polarization measurements, 
which vary with different Al contents, are linked to the formation, 
growth, and re-passivation of metastable pitting on the alloy surface. 
The corresponding evolution mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 4 [51]. The 
transformation of pitting propagation in AlxCoCrFeNi HEAs with vary­
ing Al content has been attributed to a change in the amount of the 
Cr-depleted BCC phase. The influence of alloy elements on the micro­
structure evolution and electrochemical parameters of representative 
HEAs with different solutions is summarized in Table 1.
In order to gain further insight into the effect of element variation on 
the corrosion susceptibility of AlxCoCrFeNi HEAs in NaCl solution, in- 
situ observations and high-throughput synthesis were employed by Shi 
et al. [48,50,52]. They confirmed that the corrosion begins with pitting 
and gradually evolves into the selective corrosion of the BCC phase, 
which is attributed to the formation of galvanic corrosion between the 
BCC and FCC phase [48,50]. However, they emphasized the role of Al in 
forming Cr-depleted B2 phases ((Al, Ni)-rich), which preferentially

Additionally, Wang et al. [39] also demonstrated that the corrosion 
behavior of AlxCoCrFeNi HEAs transitions from pitting corrosion to se­
lective corrosion as the Al content increases. The presence of corrosion 
products in the Al0.0 sample indicated the occurrence of pitting corro­
sion, with Co, Cr, Fe, and Ni being the primary elements affected [39]. In 
the Al0.5 and Al1.0 samples, Al and Ni were observed to be corroded, 
suggesting the presence of selective corrosion in these alloy systems. In 
the Al1.5 sample, the presence of selective corrosion was observed, with 
corrosion extending to Al, Ni, and Co, indicating a transition in the 
corroded elements from (Co, Cr, Fe, and Ni) to (Al and Ni) and finally to 
(Al, Ni, and Co) [39].
However, Raza et al. [65] demonstrated that enhancing the Al con­
tent resulted in enhanced corrosion resistance in AlxCrFeMoV HEAs. 
Unlike other Al-containing high entropy alloys, the inclusion of Al in 
AlxCrFeMoV HEAs reduced the current density in the passive region 
without reducing the pitting potential, as X-ray photoelectron spec­
troscopy (XPS) analysis confirmed the formation of Mo4+ and Cr3+, 
which stabilised the passive film and prevented pitting. The enhance­
ment of corrosion resistance exhibited by the AlxCrFeMoV alloys (x = 0, 
0.2, 0.6, 1) in conjunction with the rise in Al content is predominantly 
attributed to the elevated pitting resistance equivalent number, attrib­
utable to the presence of equiatomic Mo and Cr elements.

[66] examined the corrosion resistance of AlCoCrFeNi2.1 HEAs in 0.5 M 
H2SO4, the corresponding electrochemical parameters are illustrated in

thereby accelerating the corrosion of the eutectic HEAs, as displayed in 
Fig. 5(d).
Fu et al. [55] assessed the evolution of corrosion behavior and the 
stability of passive films in FeCoCrNiAlx alloys (where x = 0.1, 0.3) with 
varying aluminum content in 0.5 M H2SO4 solution and 0.5 M HCl so­
lution. The corrosion resistance of the HEAs in the two acid solutions 
demonstrates an inverse relationship with the increasing Al content. The 
increase in Al content in FeCoCrNiAlx alloys is beneficial to improving 
the corrosion resistance in 0.5 M H2SO4 solution, which associated with 
the formation dense oxide film. The deterioration of the corrosion 
resistance of FeCoCrNiAlx alloys in 0.5 M HCl solution with the 
increasing of Al content is predominantly attributable to the interaction 
between Cl−and Al-containing metastable ion complexes [55]. How­
ever, Kao et al. [67] demonstrated that an increase in Al content has a 
detrimental effect on the corrosion resistance of AlxCoCrFeNi alloys in 
H2SO4 solution when the temperature exceeds 27 ◦C. Conversely, an 
enhancement in corrosion resistance is observed with the incorporation 
of Al content when the temperature is below 23 ◦C.

In conclusion, the corrosion behavior of HEAs is primarily influenced 
by both structural and non-structural factors. Structural changes, 
particularly phase transformations driven by Al content, lead to selec­
tive corrosion of the BCC phase through galvanic interactions with the 
FCC phase. Additionally, non-structural factors, such as the dynamics of 
oxide film formation, dissolution, and re-passivation, significantly 
contribute to the corrosion susceptibility of HEAs. Furthermore, the 
lattice distortion energy rises with rising Al content, thereby reducing 
the protective effect of the passive film [50]. Therefore, both micro­
structural design and the stability of the oxide film must be carefully 
considered when designing HEAs for enhanced corrosion resistance.
2.2. Cr
Several investigations have proved that the corrosion susceptibility 
of alloys under extreme conditions can be regulated by the addition of 
the Cr element, which is associated with the critical concentration of Cr 
in various alloys [68].
To understand the effect of Cr incorporation, Yan et al. [56] prepared 
Al0.3CrxFeCoNi (x = 0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 1.7 and 2.0) alloys through 
arc-melting. The formation of a single FCC was observed for the 
Al0.3CrxFeCoNi alloys, with x ranging from 0 to 1.0. The appearance of a 
mixed structure of FCC and BCC/B2 is observed when Cr addition ex­
ceeds 1.5. Furthermore, an analysis was conducted on the effect of Cr on 
the corrosion susceptibility of Al0.3CrxFeCoNi alloys. The experimental 
findings demonstrated that the Cr-doping of Al0.3CrxFeCoNi alloys 
exhibited reduced corrosion susceptibility compared to that of Al0.3Fe­
CoNi alloy in a 3.5 wt % NaCl solution. The transformation of corrosion 
susceptibility of Al0.3CrxFeCoNi with different Cr content is mainly 
associated with the appearance of Cr2O3 [56,69]. A comparable corro­
sion mechanism is put forth by Lu et al. [70] for Ni38Fe20CrxMn21-0.5x­
Co21-0.5x alloys in a 0.6 M NaCl solution. It was established that the 
transformation of the corrosion susceptibility of Ni38Fe20CrxMn21-0.5x­
Co21-0.5x(x = 6,10,14 and 22) with differing Cr content is predominantly 
associated with the passivation of the Cr element. This finding indicates 
that the incorporation of Cr significantly enhances the corrosion per­
formance of the alloy, acting as a crucial positive modifier.

2.3. Ni
The corrosion behavior of HEAs is significantly influenced by the 
composition and the interaction of various elements, particularly the 
transition metals such as Co, Cr, Fe, and Ni. Among these, Ni plays a 
crucial role in the corrosion resistance and mechanical properties of 
CoCrFeNi-based HEAs.
Dong et al. [58] demonstrated that the incorporation of Ni resulted in 
the transformation of microstructure of Al0.5CoCrFeNix(x = 0.2, 0.6, 
0.8) alloys. The microscopic analysis shows that as the Ni content in­
creases, there is a transition from a single-phase BCC structure to a 
multi-phase structure consisting of BCC, FCC and B2 phases. For the 
Al0.5CoCrFeNi0 HEAs, the corrosion morphology is uniform in both acid 
and alkaline media due to the homogeneous microstructure. Then, the 
corrosion resistance of Al0.5CoCrFeNix decreases with increasing Ni 
content. This deterioration can be attributed to the formation of (Al, 
Ni)-rich B2 phases, which undergo selective dissolution in acidic solu­
tions and preferential oxidation in alkaline solutions [58].
In the study by Lopez et al. [59], the influence of the Ni ratio on the 
corrosion characteristics of AlCoCrFeNix (x = 1.0, 1.4 and 1.8) alloys in 
a simulated body fluid was investigated. The study revealed that the 
corrosion resistance of HEAs progressively improves with an increase in 
Ni content. This enhancement is attributed to the expansion of inter­
dendritic regions and the segregation of Cr within these regions, which 
facilitates the formation of a dense oxide film in the corrosive solution. 
The HEAs exhibited good stability in the simulated biological environ­
ment, which is safe for the medical device field.
The corrosion resistance of Al2CrFeCoCuTiNix(x = 0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 
2.0) HEAs was investigated by Qiu et al. [60]. The findings indicated 
that the HEAs coating demonstrated noteworthy corrosion resistance in 
both NaOH solute ion and NaCl solutions. Additionally, the corrosion 
resistance of the alloys exhibited an initial increase followed by a decline 
with increasing Ni content. This can be attributed to the excellent 
corrosion resistance of Ni. Its relatively small atomic radius means that 
when the Ni content is high, severe lattice distortions can occur within 
HEAs. These distortions impact the microstructure, thereby diminishing 
the corrosion resistance of the alloy.
Huang et al. [72] investigated the corrosion behavior of CoCr2­
Ni2AlTi, Co2Cr2Ni2AlTi and CoCr2Ni3AlTi HEAs in NaCl solution and 
H2SO4 solution. It was observed that the Co2Cr2Ni2AlTi HEAs exhibited 
remarkable corrosion resistance in both the NaCl solution and the H2SO4 
solution. The enhancement of corrosion resistance in the Co2Cr2Ni2AlTi 
HEAs was found to be associated with the formation of Cr2O3. 
Furthermore, it was illustrated that the incorporation of the Co element 
had a favourable impact on the stability of the passivation film, atten­
uating the hydrolysis of Cr3+. In contrast, the addition of Ni promotes 
the hydrolysis of Cr3+ in H₂SO₄ solution.
The current research on the effect of Ni on the corrosion resistance of 
HEAs is fragmented. The findings suggest that, within certain concen­
trations, the addition of Ni can enhance the corrosion resistance of the 
alloy. However, excessive Ni content leads to significant lattice distor­
tion and phase formation, which undermines the alloy’s corrosion per­
formance. The complexity of Ni’s effect on corrosion resistance, 
influenced by factors such as phase composition and microstructure. 
Future research should focus on optimizing the Ni content in HEAs to 
achieve optimal performance for specific applications, such as biomed­
ical devices and harsh industrial environments.
2.4. Ti
The Ti element exhibits superior corrosion resistance compared to 
Cl⁻ [73]. A number of related reports have indicated that the incorpo­
ration of an appropriate quantity of Ti into HEAs can hinder the 
movement of point defects and the breakdown of the passive film, 
thereby facilitating the formation of a more protective passive film [40,
61]. However, as the Ti content increases, element segregation is 
intensified, resulting in the formation of numerous precipitated phases, 
which negatively affects the corrosion resistance of the material [74].
There are different views on the reports that high Ti content pro­
motes the formation of multi-phase structures. Zhao et al. [61] examined 
the role of Ti/Al ratios on the corrosion resistance of Al2-xCoCrFeNiTix 
(x = 0, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, 1.0, and 1.2) HEAs in a 3.5 wt% NaCl solution. It 
was demonstrated that all Ti-doped Al2-xCoCrFeNiTix alloys exhibited a 
reduced susceptibility to pitting corrosion in comparison with the Ti-free

enhanced pitting corrosion resistance of the Ti-added alloys is attributed 
to the inhibitory effects of the Ti addition, which suppress point defect 
transport and prevent the breakdown of the passive film.
However, Jiang et al. [62] examined the combined effect of Al and Ti 
on the corrosion resistance of AlxCoCrFeNiTi1-x(x = 1.0, 0.8, 0.5) HEAs 
in NaCl, NaOH and H₂SO₄ solutions. The Al1.0CoCrFeNi and Al0.5CoCr­

NaCl and NaOH solutions. Among the three types of HEAs, the 
Al0.5CoCrFeNiTi0.5 HEAs displayed outstanding corrosion resistance in 
the H2SO4 solution. Moreover, it was emphasized that a modest amount 
of Ti added to AlxCoCrFeNiTi1-x HEAs is inadequate to mitigate the 
harmful effects of the biphasic structure and significant elemental 
segregation on corrosion resistance. It is therefore concluded that 
improvement in corrosion resistance is only possible when the atomic 
percentage of Ti surpasses that of Al. Gu et al. [73] proposed a similar 
perspective in the research exploring the corrosion resistance of 
Al0.3CoCrFeNi HEAs in NaCl solution with doping Ti elements. They also 
found that the grain size of HEAs can be reduced and the impact of the 
dual-phase structure on the corrosion resistance can be eliminated only 
when the atomic percentage of Ti exceeds that of Al, thereby enhancing 
the corrosion resistance.
Ti exhibits outstanding performance in enhancing pitting resistance, 
while Cr improves the corrosion resistance of the alloy by promoting the 
formation of a passive film. To explore the synergistic effects of Ti and Cr 
on the corrosion resistance of HEAs, Xiao et al. [75] assessed the impact 
of Cr and Ti on the corrosion performance of AlCoCuFeNi-(Cr,Ti) HEAs 
in H2SO4 solution, with the corrosion morphology shown in Fig. 9. At 
298 K, the addition of Cr resulted in higher polarization resistance and 
lower atomic radius mismatch in AlCoCuFeNi HEAs, promoting the 
formation of the corrosion-resistant BCC phase and Cr₂O₃ passive film. In 
contrast, the incorporation of Ti led to lower polarization resistance and 
higher atomic radius mismatch in FeNiCoCuAl HEAs, which favored the 
breakdown of the BCC phase and promoted the formation of the sus­
ceptible FCC phase. At 366 K, the addition of Cr increased the corrosion 
sensitivity of AlCoCuFeNi HEAs, which can be attributed to the 
decomposition of the Cr₂O₃ passive film at high temperature, preventing 
its regeneration.
In summary, the presence of Ti has been shown to result in the for­
mation of a protective passivation film of titanium oxide. The incorpo­
ration of Ti into HEAs has the potential to enhance their corrosion 
resistance. However, it is important to note that Ti has a propensity to 
react with other metals, resulting in the formation of intermetallic 
compounds. This phenomenon may result in structural inhomogeneity 
within the alloy, which could ultimately compromise the corrosion 
resistance of the alloy.

Zhang et al. [63] investigated the corrosion mechanism of 
Al0.3CoxCrFeNi(x = 0.25, 0.5, and 1.0) HEAs with varying Co contents in 
a 3.5 wt% NaCl solution. The corrosion phenomenon of Al0.3CoxCrFeNi 
HEAs is characterised by the presence of significant localised corrosion 
and pitting corrosion, as illustrated in Fig. 10. It was demonstrated that 
the corrosion resistance of Al0.3CoxCrFeNi HEAs improves with 
increasing Co content, which can be attributed to the evolution of 
microstructure. For Al0.3Co0.25CrFeNi and Al0.3Co0.5CrFeNi HEAs, the 
typical microstructure exhibited a double-phase (FCC + BCC) structure. 
More specifically, the BCC phase is constituted of a (Cr, Fe, Co)-rich A2 
phase and an (Al, Ni)-rich B2 phase. The dissolution of the BCC phase is 
associated with the occurrence of galvanic corrosion between the BCC 
and FCC phases. Furthermore, the potential difference between the A2 
phase and B2 phase resulted in the preferential dissolution of the B2 
phase, which exhibits a lower potential. In the case of the 
Al0.3Co1.0CrFeNi HEAs, only pitting corrosion was detected, which can 
be attributed to the existence of a single FCC phase. The evolution 
mechanism of corrosion behavior for Al0.3CoxCrFeNi HEAs is illustrated 
in Fig. 11.
Lee et al. [76] identified a comparable corrosion mechanism. They 
observed that Co30 HEAs exhibit a single FCC phase, whereas Co40 and 
Co50 HEAs demonstrate a dual-phase FCC + σ structure. In Ringer’s 
solution, the corrosion resistance was found to decrease with increasing 
Co content, primarily due to the presence of σ-phase enriched with Cr 
and Mo in Co40 and Co50 HEAs. This enrichment results in the formation 
of (Cr, Mo)-depleted zones, where localized damage to the passive film 
occurs.
Moreover, Liu et al. [30] analyzed the corrosion behaviour of 
Co1-xCrFeNi1+xCuy HEAs in 3.5 wt% NaCl solution. The experimental 
results showed that the formation of Cu2O oxide film on the surface of 
the Cu-rich phase compromises the integrity of the Cr2O3 passive film. 
After the initial corrosion of the Cu2O film, the Cu-rich phase and the 
regions with high Cu content were exposed. Subsequently, the Cu-rich 
phase underwent preferential corrosion as a consequence of the poten­
tial difference between the two phases, as illustrated in Fig. 12. Apart 
from copper, the other elements form metal oxides that exhibit excellent 
corrosion resistance. Furthermore, the researchers demonstrated that 
reducing the Co content and increasing the Ni content can mitigate the 
segregation of Cu, thereby reducing the corrosion susceptibility of HEAs. 
However, their exploration of synergistic effects remains limited, with 
insufficient investigation into the interactions among Co, Cu, Ni, and Cr. 
Additionally, Wu et al. [77] also indicated that the synergistic effect 
between the Co and other elements leads to the formation of complex 
oxides, which enhances the corrosion resistance of Fe40N20Co20Cr20 
HEAs in H₂SO₄ solution.

et al. [78] examined the impact of N on microbiologically induced

by pressure-induced melting. They indicated that N alloying inhibited 
pitting and moderated the corrosion rate of HEAs attacked by Desulfo­
vibrio vulgaris. The enrichment of N in the passive layer resulted in an 
increase in the content of Cr2O3 and a reduction in the defect density 
within the passive layer, and the corrosion mechanism is illustrated in

3. Effect of heat treatment on the corrosion of HEAs
The majority of HEAs utilised in experimental procedures are cast 
materials, which exhibit inadequate microstructure uniformity and a 
propensity for the emergence of defects such as cracks and pores 
[83–85]. The appropriate heat treatment process has the capacity to 
refine the grain, improve the microstructure density, and render the 
microstructure of the alloyed material more uniform [86–89]. Table 2
summarizes the relevant corrosion resistance parameters of some HEAs 
at different heat treatment temperatures.
By altering the heat treatment temperature, the phase structure and 
morphology of the precipitates in the alloy can be adjusted, thereby 
influencing its corrosion resistance [90,91]. Guo et al. [92] investigated 
the impact of heat treatment temperature (1100 ◦C, 1150 ◦C, 1185 ◦C, 
1200 ◦C) for 2h on the corrosion susceptibility of AlCoCrFeNiSi HEAs in 
a 3.5 wt% NaCl solution. It was demonstrated that the phase trans­
formation doesn’t occur with different heat treatment temperatures, 
except for changes in microstructure morphology. The typical corrosion 
morphology of HEAs after the electrochemical experiment in a 3.5 wt% 
NaCl solution is illustrated in Fig. 16. As-cast alloy and the samples 
heat-treated at 1185 ◦C for 2 h (Fig. 16(a–d)) exhibited obvious pitting 
corrosion. In addition, corrosion deposits such as irregular blocks were 
observed on the surface of heat-treated samples at 1100 ◦C and 1150 ◦C, 
which may retard the corrosion rate. The corrosive morphology is dis­
played in Fig. 17. Pitting corrosion was infrequently observed in all 
HEAs. The results indicated that the (Al, Ni)-rich dendrite phase facili­
tated the formation of a passive film. Consequently, due to the occur­
rence of a weak passivation phenomenon, the samples treated at 1100 ◦C 
demonstrated the best corrosion resistance.
Additionally, Guo et al. [93] examined the impact of phase compo­
sition changes, induced by heat treatment, on the corrosion suscepti­
bility of AlCoCrFeNiTi0.5 HEAs in a 3.5 wt% NaCl solution. They 
discovered that, contrary to the expected trend of the BCC phase volume 
fraction, the corrosion resistance of the HEAs decreased with increasing 
heat treatment temperature. This reduction in corrosion resistance was 
mainly attributed to the growth of grain and phase boundaries, as well as 
the potential difference between the dendritic FCC phase and the 
interdendritic BCC phase. Zhang et al. [94] also demonstrated that the 
evolution of corrosion resistance of the AlFeNiCoCuCr HEAs with 
different heat treatment conditions (as-cast, remelted and annealed) in a 
3.5 wt% NaCl solution was associated with the morphology trans­
formation of dendrite and interdendritic phases. It was observed that the 
corrosion resistance of the HEAs significantly improved after annealing 
treatment. This enhancement can be attributed to the growth of den­
drites post-annealing, which exhibited larger sizes compared to those in 
the as-cast and remelted conditions.

resistance of the CoCrFeNiW0.5 HEAs at different annealing tempera­
tures was linked to changes in the morphology and volume of the μ 
phase. Specifically, numerous needle-like μ phases were observed within 
the FCC matrix after heat treatment at 1000 ◦C. However, when the 
annealing temperature was increased to 1200 ◦C, these μ phases dis­
solved into the matrix, resulting in galvanic corrosion between the FCC 
matrix and the remaining μ phase.
The annealing process has a significant impact on the microstructure 
and corrosion resistance of alloys, particularly under varying tempera­
ture and time conditions. Dai et al. [97] studied the effects of annealing 
temperature and duration on the corrosion susceptibility of FeCoCr­
NiMo0.1 in 1 M NaCl solution. The results show that the corrosion 
resistance of FeCoCrNiMo0.1 HEAs was enhanced following annealing at 
900 ◦C, with the duration of the process varying from 3 min to 60 min. 
However, it was found that the corrosion resistance was deteriorated 
when the annealing temperature was altered from 900 ◦C to 1050 ◦C for 
a period of 60 min. This can be attributed to the grain size growth 
observed at annealing temperatures exceeding 900 ◦C, which increased 
corrosion susceptibility of FeCoCrNiMo0.1 HEAs.
It is imperative to assess the corrosion susceptibility of HEAs in acidic 
solutions, with the exception of corrosion susceptibility in NaCl solu­
tions. The corrosion susceptibility of annealed AlCoCrFeNi HEAs in 0.5 
M H2SO4 aerated aqueous solution was investigated by Yen et al. [98]. 
They found that a more uniform passivation film containing hydroxide 
was formed after annealing. Furthermore, the researchers provided a 
detailed explanation of the corrosion mechanism of the annealed 
AlCoCrFeNi HEAs, as shown in Fig. 18. The Cr-rich hydroxide passive 
film became denser and more stable due to the formation of Fe(OH)3 and 
Co(OH)3, which inhibited the release of Fe3+, Co3+, Ni2+ and Al3+, 
forming the passive region as shown in Fig. 18(b). However, the Cr-rich 
hydroxide was further oxidized to CrO4
2−, then dissolved in the solution 
and caused the Cr content on the passive film to decrease, as depicted in 
Fig. 18(c). Cu is a less stable element, which makes it less resistant to 
corrosion in electrolytes, especially in solutions containing Cl−
[99,
100].
Based on the above discussions, it has been clearly demonstrated that 
corrosion properties are strongly influenced by both microstructure and 
composition. The change in corrosion resistance of HEAs after heat 
treatment is primarily associated with volume changes in the BCC and 
FCC phases and local element segregation. Consequently, the homoge­
nisation effect of heat treatment could minimise the chances of localised 
corrosion, thus improving the corrosion resistance.
4. The relationship between passive film and corrosion 
resistance of material
4.1. The composition and structure of passive film
In a broad context, the passive film on the metal surface can be 
considered a distinct phase. The formation process of this passive film on 
the alloy surface mainly involves the reaction of cations from elements 
such as Al, Co, Cr, Fe, and other alloying elements, leading to the gen­
eration of oxides and hydroxides. The variation in element distribution 
within the passive film is principally attributable to differences in 
oxidation potential and diffusion rates. However, it is also affected by 
factors such as the corrosive medium, pH level, and temperature.
To comprehensively investigate the passivation mechanisms of HEAs 
in acidic and saline environments, Lu et al. [102] conducted a com­
parison of the corrosion resistance between CoCrFeMnNi and 
Fe50Mn30Co10Cr10 dual-phase HEAs in a 3.5 wt% NaCl solution. The 
findings indicated that the corrosion product film on the Fe50Mn30­
Co10Cr10 dual-phase HEA exhibited a thickness of 12 nm, a value that 
was significantly higher than the 3 nm film observed on the CoCrFeMnNi 
alloy. Furthermore, the corrosion product film on the CoCrFeMnNi HEA 
displayed a lower Mn content and a higher Cr and Fe content in com­
parison to that observed in the Fe50Mn30Co10Cr10 dual-phase HEA.

5. Summary and future prospects
The corrosion behavior of HEAs is primarily influenced by both 
structural and non-structural factors. Structural changes, particularly 
phase transformations driven by the addition of different elements and 
various content. The occurrence of corrosion is mainly associated with 
the formation of galvanic corrosion between the BCC and FCC phases. 
Additionally, the potential difference of a (Cr, Fe, Co)-rich A2 phase and 
an (Al, Ni)-rich B2 phase of BCC phase also led to the selective corrosion. 
For the non-structural factors, such as the dynamics of oxide film for­
mation, dissolution, and re-passivation, significantly contribute to the 
corrosion susceptibility of HEAs. Furthermore, the lattice distortion 
caused by the different atomic dimensions of the constituent primary 
elements within HEA has a significant effect on the stability of the 
passive film. Consequently, both microstructural design and the stability 
of the oxide film must be meticulously considered when designing HEAs 
for enhanced corrosion resistance. The change in corrosion resistance of 
HEAs can be explained by the change in microstructure and the for­
mation of a dense oxide film after the addition of Cr and Al elements.
Furthermore, HEAs often exhibit sluggish diffusion, which can affect 
how corrosion products develop and evolve. In some cases, this sluggish 
diffusion helps to slow down the overall corrosion process, while in 
others, it may promote localized degradation if corrosion products 
cannot redistribute evenly. The grain boundaries and phase segregation 
in HEAs also play a significant role at the microscale. These features can 
act as corrosion initiation sites, particularly if certain phases are more 
anodic compared to the matrix, leading to selective dissolution.
Overall, the corrosion mechanisms in HEAs are governed by the 
interplay of localized galvanic corrosion, passive film stability, sluggish 
diffusion, and phase distribution, all of which are influenced by the 
complex chemistry and structural characteristics of these alloys. In order 
to enhance the applicability of HEAs, future research on the corrosion 
susceptibility of HEAs may concentrate on the following areas, building 
upon the investigation of corrosion characteristics. 
(1) Corrosion mechanism of HEAs at micro-scale
Further investigation of the micro-scale corrosion characteristics of 
HEAs will be a hot topic in the future, which will require the use of 
advanced surface analysis techniques. High-throughput electrochemical 
characterization of micro-corrosion is useful for monitoring and char­
acterizing the electrochemical behavior of material and metal corrosion 
and dissolution at nanometer and sub-micron levels at high speed and 
high throughput. Furthermore, the intricate corrosion processes and 
corrosion mechanisms of HEAs at the micro-scale interface require 
investigation, including the formation and dissolution of the passive film 
and the evolution of the HEA/solution interface. Additionally, in-situ 
corrosion characterization under multi-factor coupling is also a 
research hotspot in the future. It is essential to examine the influence of 
high entropy, lattice distortion, sluggish diffusion and the "cocktail" ef­
fect on the corrosion susceptibility of HEAs, which will provide key 
technical support for quality control, process optimization and property 
evaluation of HEAs. 
(2) Evolution of corrosion behavior under various environments
The exceptional properties of HEA expand the range of applications. 
These applications primarily encompass welding and brazing filler ma­
terials, biomedical materials, nuclear structural materials, optical 
transmission materials, heat-resistant or wear-resistant coatings, 
hydrogen storage materials, etc. Consequently, there is a need for a 
systematic investigation into the evolution of corrosion susceptibility in 
service environments. The above can provide detailed information on 
stress corrosion cracking, corrosion fatigue and other local corrosion 
performance of HEAs. During the analysis of corrosion data, the estab­
lishment of high-throughput data collection and machine-learning 
technology will also be a hot topic. 
(3) Efficiency of component design and preparation of HEAs
