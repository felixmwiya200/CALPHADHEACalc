6. High entropy oxides as catalysts
6.1. Maximized disorder as a descriptor for catalytic activity
Fig. 11 approaches the concept of maximized disorder, formulated as
lattice strain, from a catalytic point of view, where in previous studies
the idea of enhancing the oxygen mobility of a metal-oxide system
was studied through introducing dopants where Alkhoori et al. [171]
and Alketbi et al. [172] studied both pure and metal doped CeO2 sys-
tems, by taking CeO2, as a starting model for a low entropy oxide system
since it consists of 1 cation (Ce) and has two oxidation states (+4 and
+3), it is known for its ability to store oxygen, which means that it
has intrinsic defects (oxygen vacancies) [173,174]. Adding alio-valent
dopants such as Sm and Cu to the pure CeO2 shifted the whole system
to the range of medium entropy metal oxides, with the fact that it
could improve the oxygen mobility directions. This was implemented
through creating extra oxygen vacancies near Cu dopant atoms, the lat-
ter proved through DFT studies, that they are reducing the energy of ox-
ygen vacancy formation (Evf) [171]. Therefore, high entropy oxides
(e.g.: CeLaYSmPr)O), as suggested in Fig. 12(A), are anticipated to
have improved catalytic activity since they will have different active
sites if they were composed from higher number of alio-valent elements
with holding the symmetry of single phase (e.g.: Fluorite structure). The
population of oxygen defects (Ov) in pure/doped ceria was studied thor-
oughly in different studies by experimental and computational methods
including Raman spectroscopy and Density Functional Theory, whereas
the lattice oxygen mobility (pre-condition of Ov formation) was studied
using 18O TIE experiments [172]. It was well-reported in the literature,
as shown in Fig. 12(A), that the Raman spectra of CeO2 has a main

6.2. Thermal catalysis
Catalysis is known for its essential role in the chemical industry par-
ticularly in facilitating the production of added-value hydrocarbons,
chemicals and fuels (e.g. CH4, H2, CH3OH) [30]. Besides, catalysts are
being studied to be employed for the removal of fuel impurities, such
as sulfur, towards producing cleaner fuels with desirable properties
(e.g. ignition, viscosity, etc.) [175]. There is a major challenge when it
comes to efﬁcient metal catalysts design, mainly in overcoming the co-
hesive energy that drives co-domain formation [172] and sintering at el-
evated temperatures [176]. Therefore, there is an immense effort
tailoring catalytic systems that stabilize noble metal single atoms and
nanoparticles at high temperature. In this section, high entropy oxides
are being discussed in the catalysis ﬁeld where they have shown their
superiority in thermal/photo and electro-catalysis for a versatile spec-
trum of reaction spanning from oxidation to reduction.
6.3. CO oxidation
Chen et al. [44] reported a successful trial of synthesizing (0.3 wt%
PtNiMgCuCoZn)Ox, an entropy stabilized oxide, which had rock-salt sin-
gle phase, by the method of Pt co-deposition. In the synthesis procedure,
the transition metals precursors were dissolved in distilled water and
stirred, then the noble metal precursor was added to the prepared solu-
tion (one pot synthesis). The material exhibited high dispersion of Pt
which conﬁrmed that the six (6) cations crystalized homogenously to
form a high entropy oxide. The study showed that elevating by changing
the heat related synthesis condition (the calcination temperature), it
can give rise to a uniform phase in the material, but at the same time
can affect negatively the catalytic activity for CO oxidation due to
sintering, as shown in Fig. 13(A). The as-prepared catalyst shows supe-
rior performance compared to the heat-treated material and could
achieve complete CO conversion at 155 °C. The study also pointed out
at the effect of changing the synthesis method on the catalysts, and
proved, through BET studies, as shown in Fig. 13(B), that the co-
precipitated (NiMgCuCoZn)Ox had higher surface area (~28 m2 g-1)
than the ball milled (NiMgCuCoZn)Ox (~2 m2 g-1) due to the recrystalli-
zation of the material upon calcination. The synthesis technique was in-

6.5. CO2 hydrogenation
Reducing CO2 to CO by H2 was also investigated using Noble
Metals-HEOs. On the contrary to the used HEO catalysts, Chen et al.
[53] prepared (2–5 wt%) Ru/Pt-(NiMgCuZnCo)O catalyst under ambient
conditions, and showed the effect of the gained maximized entropy on
the loading and dispersion of the noble metals. Although 5wt%(Heat
Treated at 500 °C) Pt-(NiMgCuZnCo)O was the only catalyst that could
reduce up to 47.8% of CO2 , but all the synthesized systems exhibited
high selectivity to CO 95%, and high catalytic stability that could reach
up to 12 hours at 500 °C without any signiﬁcant fall backs in their per-
formance. This result was justiﬁed using HAADF-STEM characterization
that showed neither aggregations nor segregations of the loaded Pt/Ru
in (NiMgCuZnCo)O, and the HEOs’ latent properties that enable them
to perform actively at harsh temperature conditions, and behave as a
stabilizing support which disperse the active noble metals as
nanoclusters (2–3 nm) and single atoms. Towards CO as a main product,
the reported systems can be further employed to be active for the re-
verse water gas shift reaction, or the systems compositions can be
tuned and be exploited to convert CO2 to CH4.

6.8. CH4 partial oxidation
A technical report that was published by Stanford University for
Global Climate and Energy project [137] demonstrated the utilization of
HEO for CH4 partial oxidation. It showed that (MgNiZnCuCo)Ox,
(MgTiZnCuCo)Ox (AlFeCrGaTi)Ox, (FeCoMgNiMn)Ox, (MgCaNiCoFeMn)
Ox and Cu nanoparticles supported by the previous catalysts resulted in
oxidizing CH4 partially to CO2 and CO in temperatures that ranged be-
tween 25–500 °C. The research was targeting producing methanol out
of CH4, but it concluded that the used elements played an important
role in determining the route of the reaction where the excessive amount
of active O in the tested HEOs redirected the selectivity from methanol to
carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide. Besides, the metal-oxygen bond-
ing, and the condition of the high temperature was able only to activate
the methane molecule without any signiﬁcant effect on producing the de-
sired reaction yield.
6.9. Alcohol oxidation
Feng et al. [151] prepared an improved mesoporous lamellar high en-
tropy oxides catalyst (CoNiCuMgZnO) which successfully facilitated the
oxidation of benzyl alcohol and converted 98% of the aromatic alcohol
to mainly benzoic acid, benzaldehyde, and benzyl benzoate at 120 °C in
2 h only. The reported catalyst showed superior performance in terms
of stability and activity compared to Au/Pd-TiO2 that achieved a maxi-
mum conversion of 91.6% of benzyl alcohol in a four times longer duration
period. The prominent catalytic activity was explained by the existence of
the increased oxygen vacancies sites in the structure of the holey HEO
which was synthesized by an anchoring-merging process with graphene
oxide that in its turn increased the number of the reduced sites of the cat-
alyst and enhanced the hydrogen and M-O bonds between the functional
group of the alcoholic molecules (-OH) and the exposed surface oxygen
atoms and reduced catalytic sites. In addition, the surface area
(~42 m2 g-1) and the pore volume of 0.106 cm3 g-1 of the novel material
contributed to the better activity due to the strengthening of the energy
of adsorption of benzyl alcohol. The study focused also on recording the
effect of changing the composition of the elements, where decreasing all
the elements by 10% increased the time required to achieve the chemical
equilibrium by one hour. On the other hand, decreasing the composition
of one element at a time by 30% affected the selectivity that was shifted to
benzaldehyde and incremented the time needed to achieve the chemical
equilibrium by 7 times.

6.11. Photocatalysis
High entropy oxides were also reported by Edalati et al. [150] for the
application of photocatalysis, in particular for hydrogen evolution dur-

vestigated for this purpose. The catalyst had mixed phases that were
revealed to be 60 mol% monoclinic perovskite and the rest was ortho-
rhombic perovskite. The high entropy material exhibited a band gap
value which was within the range of the visible light (~2.9 eV) when
its cations oxides possessed band gaps between 3.1–5.7 eV. The material
was tested for the desired reaction by exposing it for 180 minutes to UV
light. It was observed that after the 30th minute, the time of exposure
and the released hydrogen were linearly increasing.
Another recently published photocatalytic application was reported

timeframe of approximately 180 min and reduce 100% of Cr(VI) to Cr
(III) in a time duration of 90–100 min. Cr and MB are considered as
main contamination sources of drinking water and converting them
to less toxic species by cost and time effective materials is a challenge
to be addressed. It should be noted that the reported HEO were able
to function efﬁciently by exposing them to sunlight due to their ability
to absorb high wavelengths.

7. Computational studies
This section presents the computational studies that have been
employed so far to unravel the properties which are directly related to
the structure and catalytic performance of the high entropy oxides.
Modelling high entropy oxides unit cells with taking into consideration
all the possible conﬁgurations with the intrinsic oxygen vacancies is still
not reported in literature; though such a study is expected to enlighten
the HEO potential as catalysts. To the best of our knowledge, over the
last ﬁve years, few computational studies of high entropy oxides were
published in the ﬁeld, highlighting the difﬁculty dealing with such com-
plex structures. As explained previously, the population and mobility of
the oxygen vacancies play a crucial role in improving the performance
of the catalysts. Density Functional Theory (DFT) is a powerful tool
used to validate the experimental results by studying the low (e.g.
CeO2 and CeSmCuO) and medium entropy (e.g. BaSrCoFeO) [193] ox-
ides at their atomistic level and calculate the energy of formation of ox-
ygen vacancies, located at the most preferable sites, and their behavior
under strain. Up to date publications show a clear gap in describing
high entropy oxides’ oxygen vacancies quantitatively by DFT. In what
follows, the use of computational tools on difference aspects of HEO is
discussed; from the point of formation (mixing) to intrinsic properties
(e.g. band gap).
7.1. HEO mixing behavior
A case study published by Zhong et al. [194] where a new computa-
tional approach applied by CALculation of PHAse Diagram (CALPHAD)
was adapted to evaluate the difference between the mixing in the
high entropy ceramics and the high entropy alloys on the simple perov-
skite model (LaMnO3±δ). The study deﬁned clearly that mixing in
HEOs stands for the disorder of the cations, anions and defects sites
while in the HEAs it only refers to the disorder of the main constituents
that contributes with more than 5 mol% in the material. The study
highlighted the difﬁculty of recording the mixing in high entropy ce-
ramics in general due to the different contribution of the lattice constit-
uents (cations and anions) and the lattice defect chemistry.
7.2. Phase stability
From a thermodynamic point a view, Anand et al. [26] conducted a
computational study and investigated the effect of decreasing/changing
the number of components/ composition of HEO (NiMgCoCuZn)O on
the enthalpy and conﬁgurational entropy through optimized lattice
conﬁgurations of cations and anions. In terms of the compositions,
(CoCuMgNi)O and (CoMgNiZn)O achieved the lowest mixing en-
thalpies. And, in terms of the number of components, it was observed
that adding Ca to the system as in (CaCoCuNiZn)O increased the mixing
enthalpy which indicated its favorability to form secondary phases
[195]. It was proved that the HEO system is entropy stabilized since
the enthalpy contribution was positive and signiﬁcantly low. Decreasing
the number of cations from 5 to 4 affects directly on lowering the
mixing entropy and increasing the enthalpy, which requires greater
temperature compensation to stabilize the phase of the system and
drive it to lower free energy. Changing the composition to include Ca

Another computational study by Jiang et al. [87] investigated the local
distortion in the pyrochlore type of HEO Nd2(Ta0.2Sc0.2Sn0.2Hf0.2Zr0.2)
2O7 and Nd2(Ti0.2Nb0.2Sn0.2Hf0.2Zr0.2)2O7+x where modeling with pair
distribution function, special quasirandom structures, DFT-Ab initio mo-
lecular dynamics, Reverse Monte Carlo method, and Metropolis Monte
Carlo simulations were presented. The main ﬁndings of the study
were as follows: 1) the average structure of the simulated materials
was orthorhombic (space-group Imma), 2) the participating cations de-
termine the nature of the local distortion, as TiO6 was found to experi-

was found to have a uniform and highly disordered structure. Further-
more, the study emphasized on the importance of local distortion com-
putational studies that will enable tailoring the properties of HEO.
7.3. Adsorption energies studies
Feng et al. [151] augmented their experimental results (Thermal Ca-
talysis: Alcohol Oxidation section) with performing computational
analysis on the atomistic level of the material and density functional
theory calculations by using Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package
(VASP) as a functioned tool to investigate the adsorption energy of ben-
zyl alcohol molecules that is affected directly by the number of the
existing oxygen vacancies in the novel holey lamellar high entropy
oxide catalyst. The simulated FCC surface (111) was obtained after opti-
mizing the atomic positions by the conjugate gradient algorithm. It
consisted from 160 total number of atoms (16 atoms for each of the 5
cations and 80 atoms for the anion), with a lattice parameter of
4.23 Å. The research showed the signiﬁcant role of the increased oxygen
vacancies sites in enhancing the performance of the holey catalyst (Ea
benzyl molecule = 1.43 eV) compared to the bulk HEO (Ea=0.27 eV)
which clearly demonstrates the higher stability of the molecule on the
holey slab as shown in Fig. 18(A) and (B).
