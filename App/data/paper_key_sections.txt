This review presents the structural elements and discusses the great potential of high entropy oxides (HEO) as
promising catalysts. A critical comparison is provided with the medium and low entropy metal oxides in terms
of the important functionality of HEO to exhibit higher oxygen mobility and withhold high population of oxygen
vacancies as well as leading to high metal dispersion. This review study critically compares the performance of
the thermal, electro- and photo- high entropy oxides catalysts with the conventional metal oxides and demon-
strates their superiority over them while discussing the governing characteristics of HEO. The HEO complex
structure is highlighted using ab initio calculations on understanding and tuning their electronic/structural prop-
erties. Catalysts’ design criteria and direction in the studies of the HEO as catalysts for energy and sustainability
are proposed.

Entropy stabilized materials attracted the attention of the scientiﬁc
community as it is reﬂected in the review papers on the HEOs [19–22]
and the High Entropy Ceramics (HEC) (e.g.: borides, nitrides, oxides, sil-
icides, sulﬁdes and carbides) [23–25] already published in the open lit-
erature. It should be mentioned that the present work scope is the
correlation of the high entropy oxides (HEO) to their catalytic perfor-
mance in the ﬁelds of thermal, electro- and photo- catalysis. Compari-
son of the HEO with the conventional metal oxides is herein presented
and their potential superiority over the ﬁrst is demonstrated and criti-
cally discussed in terms of their structural features. In the herein
study, only a short coverage of the synthesis methods is presented, as
it has been the subject of other critical review articles [20,23,25]. The
existing literature covers mostly the synthesis aspects and challenges
as well as properties such as magnetic, optical, thermoelectric
[20,23,25].
From a catalytic application point of view, designing high entropy
oxide-based materials is a ﬁeld which is not yet fully developed. To-
wards expanding the HEO engineering space a strategic design is re-
quired, where some important parameters are accounted, as explained
in what follows: (a) during the formation of HEO the enthalpy of mixing
and the conﬁgurational entropy dictate the potential of a real HEO for-
mation; high enthalpy implies potential segregation (or clustering)
whereas going to higher number of cations increases the entropy stabi-
lization of these structures. The entropy stabilization phenomenon can
be checked by performing calcination treatment of the material at low
and high temperatures and validate the presence or not of a single
phase [26]. This type of experiment has been proposed for both rock-
salt structures (R-HEO) and ﬂuorite (F-HEO) ones. (b) In the selection
of the constituent elements two important parameters are the valence
of the cations and their coordination environment compatibility. By co-
ordination environment compatibility, it is meant that the size of the
cations should be appropriate for the oxygen sub-lattice whereas the
structural tolerance factor should also be fulﬁlled [7,27].At the same
time, the valence of the constituent cations should be such as that
charge balance is maintained. Along these lines, there is a lot of litera-
ture exploration, despite that HEO is a new family of materials, on com-
bining cations with the same valence. Expanding towards the direction
of combining cations with valence difference can really take us to new
materials with unexplored so far properties and catalytic performance
due to the so called ‘charge distortion’. The latter is a phenomenon
due to the uneven electron cloud distribution between the metals
with the different valence and the oxygen. Such an electron cloud dis-
tortion is anticipated to impact different catalytic reactions, through dif-
ferent pathways, such as H2S removal [28], CO2 reduction to different
hydrocarbons [29,30], hydrocarbon reforming. In the above mentioned
reactions, HEO can be used as standalone catalysts or catalytic supports
(decorated with active metal) due to their intrinsic lattice distortion.
The major focus on the existing open literature on the HEO is placed
on the magnetic [31–34] and electrical [35–37] properties so far.

Changing the elemental composition or combination directly affects
the structural properties of the material such as: the appearance of
minor phases in the major crystal structure. Sarkar et al. [22] re-
explained the dominant role of the conﬁgurational entropy through tak-
ing the example of NiMgCoCuZnO, which is a rock-salt high entropy
oxide, that consists of ﬁve (5) cations where Cu and Zn are responsible
for increasing the endothermicity of the system [26] and at the same
time discarding any of the other cations will take back the system to a
lower conﬁgurational entropy (1.39R) which will be evident from the
phase separations that will take place in the material [7]. To summarize
the above example, entropy has more inﬂuence on the entropy stabi-
lized system and it is agreed that maximizing the conﬁgurational en-
tropy of the system by using equimolar amounts of each cation in
most of the cases overcomes the Gibbs free energy of formation and
this eventually compensates any positive enthalpic contributions as
the total disorder of the oxygen anions is counted zero [10,17,44].

Besides, the ability to retain back the single phase again could be

Among the elements of the periodic table, Co, Cu, Mg, Ni, and Zn are con-
sidered as qualiﬁed candidates to be blended in a uniform high entropy
oxide system. However, Cu and Zn have lower compatibility, relative to
the others (different crystal structures) which limits their stability. And
as the stabilizing effect of entropy is dominant at high temperature, this
might be viewed as an obstacle for potential applications, especially the
ones at low to moderate temperature ranges. In HEA, systems are stable
in a wide temperature range, not only by entropy but also compatible
chemical composition [49]. The new class of oxides grasped the
attention to unravel their thermal behavior [50], electrical conductivi-
ties [51], band gaps [52], as well as their catalytic activity [44,53,54].

(HEA), based on the concepts of alloys, reveals outstanding properties
of the counterpart oxides. As discussed by Rost et al. [7], considering

to HEA system due to the existence of arranged arrays of oxygen atoms.

3.2. Entropy stabilized oxides (ESO) vs. high entropy oxides (HEO)
Also, it is important to draw the attention that even though “high-
entropy and entropy-stabilization are often used interchangeably, they
are fundamentally different” [17]. The concept of high entropy reﬂects
that the system simply has high conﬁgurational entropy without any
consideration to the endothermicity or exothermicity of the system
[7,17,19]. However, entropy stabilization indicates that the system
needs to possess both high conﬁgurational entropy and positive en-
thalpy of formation (+ΔH) which means that the entropy drives the
thermodynamic state of stability [17]. It should also be noted that
many studies reported did not verify the entropic stabilization of their
materials since the reversibility tests and the Differential Scanning Cal-
orimetry (DSC) characterization were not discussed [45,56]. Overall,
conﬁgurational disorder gives a way to discover new different phases
of crystalline matter and ﬁnd new opportunities for property
engineering.
3.3. Reversibility
Based on Rost et al. [7] study, reversibility of the ESO was deﬁned by
subjecting the material’s pellets to calcination in air (~ 700–1100 °C)
and following it by quenching at room temperature. The sample was
characterized by X-ray diffraction as shown in Fig. 6 where the detected
peaks demonstrated the existence of minor phases at lower tempera-
tures of the heat treatment. Multi-phases of rocksalt and tenorite were
observed after 700 °C, as the tenorite peaks intensity decreased with
the signiﬁcant increase of temperature to crystalize into rocksalt single
phase between 850 and 900 °C. Since reversibility is a requirement for
entropy driven transitions, low temperature should transform the pel-
lets back to multi-phase state, as demonstrated in Fig. 4.
3.4. High vs. medium vs. low entropy oxides

systems as Cantor et al. [11] and Yeh at al. [12] had established. Low en-
tropy oxides are deﬁned to be composed out of less than 3 different
cations, where their conﬁgurational entropy values are less than 1 R
[41]. Similarly, for the medium entropy oxides systems, the conﬁgura-
tional disorder values usually range between 1 R up to 1.5 R which indi-

5. Entropy stabilized oxides: Unveiling the ‘real’ structure towards
understanding the properties
This section provides an overview of the analytical tools, as reported
in the open literature towards investigating structural, morphological,
and textural features of the HEO along with important surface proper-
ties in their role as catalysts. All the published studies reported similar
analytical tools of characterization for exploring high entropy oxides
and entropy stabilized oxides as shown in Fig. 8. The insights of the au-
thors are provided for unveiling more characteristics and functionalities
of such complex structures as HEO. In addition, from the catalysis point
of view, a full understanding of the microstructure/chemical bonding is
needed so to establish the structure-performance correlation. Towards
this, the following suite of analysis is highlighted.
5.1. Unveiling the not-so-perfect but real structure/microstructure
X-Ray Diffraction veriﬁes the crystallinity, and the existence of a sin-
gle phase, whereas it allows a ﬁrst calculation of the primary crystallite
size and of the lattice strain (caused by the dopants presence) using
Scherrer equation [154] and Hall-Williamson method [155], respec-
tively. Though, it has to be mentioned that XRD provides information
only for the average long-range periodicity, whereas local order coordi-
nation can be obtained using X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAFS,
XANES). XAFS is element speciﬁc and thus coordination and defects
can be probed [156]. Both, XRD and EXAFS try to analyze the M-O
bond environment; for instance in the case of a rare earth HEO contain-
ing Ce, the focus is on the Ce-O and dopant (D)-O, where D: Gd, Sm, La, Y
or Pr -O , interatomic distances with the assumption of the ﬂuorite space
group symmetry. Though, this is valid only for <20 at.% doping level. So,
in the case of HEO it is challenging to explore the distortions introduced
to the ﬂuorite cell. There are many reported cases of double ﬂuorite
structure with intense angular distortions (bond buckling phenomena),
as traced by EXAFS, where XRD failed to detect it (single F phase was
detected).

the different types of oxygen vacant sites (surface, subsurface, dimer,
trimer, and cluster vacancies). This way the elements in a HEO structure
can be categorized based on their potential to accommodate oxygen va-
cant sites in the nearest neighbor (NN) or next nearest neighbor (NNN).
Also, Raman spectroscopy is a powerful tool for the structural studies
of metal oxides and totally complimentary to the XRD technique. In ad-
dition, Raman Spectroscopy helps to investigate the oxygen vacancies in
the lattice and differentiate contributions from intrinsic and extrinsic
defects (defect region 250 cm-1 and 500–600 cm-1, particularly for the
rare earths HEO) including oxygen vacancies and dopant hetero-
phases (e.g. MO8). Furthermore, the Raman bands blue/red shift are
linked to increase/decrease in the lattice induced by the presence
of larger/smaller cations in the HEO lattice structure. Lattice distortion
is indicative of the M-O bond environment/distance and thus it can be
correlated to the oxygen vacancies (Ov) formation. Calculation of the
energy of the Ov formation (Evf) can be performed using DFT calcula-
tions. The Evf values can subsequently be linked to the Ov population
(Raman spectroscopy studies). There are many reports in the literature
where the Evf has been used as catalytic activity descriptor [160].
High Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HRTEM) is used
to detect the presence of any micro/nano domains and their interface
giving thus insights of the microstructure of the material. Selected
Area Electron Diffraction (SAED) is complimentary to the XRD tech-
nique towards evaluating the crystalline phases in the HEO.

taken as the plates diameter or ﬂakes thickness but not as their length.

5.5. Oxidation states
X-Ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is a beneﬁcial tool to reveal
the existing oxidations states of all the elements in the material’s surface
and evaluate if the dopant is iso-valent (IVD), lower valent (LVD) or
higher valent (HVD) in the material. In addition, the surface atomic
composition (at.%) as received using XPS is very useful information to
evaluate any element preference to reside on the surface (selective sur-
face enrichment). Such type of materials behavior is anticipated to have
a signiﬁcant impact on the catalytic performance, as the surface compo-
sition of the HEO will be different to the one in the bulk.
5.6. Transient techniques
Transient techniques is a wide family of techniques where changing
the probing gas can provide a plethora of information. For instance, H2-
Temperature Programmed Reduction (H2-TPR) can be employed to
study the redox properties of the material, a very important property
as the oxygen mobility is of pivotal importance for many catalytic reac-
tions. In addition, transient isotopic isothermal exchange experiments
(TIIE) using 18O can be a unique way to probe the changes in the surface
and bulk of the oxygen sub-lattice(s) of the HEO. Through this type of
experiment, analysis of the transient kinetic rates of 16O/18O exchange is
performed and the 18O2, 16O18O and 16O2 species evolution signal is moni-
tored giving ultimately crucial information about the oxygen mobility
on the surface/subsurface and bulk [164]. The reactions that describe
the exchange are the following:

5.7. Surface acidity/basicity
In catalysis (thermal, electro- and photo-) the surface acidity and ba-
sicity has been reported to play key role in C-C cracking, dehydration,
dehydrogenation, proton transfer reactions, [165,166] etc. Two are the
predominant approaches for explaining the surface acidity and basicity;
Lewis and Brönsted-Lowry theories. Lewis acid/base is an electron ac-
ceptor/donor, whereas a Bronsted-Lowry acid/base is a proton done/ac-
ceptor, respectively. There is a plethora of acid-base chemistries that can
take place as a result of the following: (a) the oxygen vacancies are
Lewis base sites (due to the unpaired electrons left upon their forma-
tion), (b) a metal oxide surface can be catalytically tuned through sub-
stitutional doping with low (or high) valence dopant (LVD or HVD)
which increases the Lewis acidity/basicity of the surface [167], respec-
tively. This type of activity is linked with lowering of the activation en-
ergy of the C-H bond and activation of CH4. This process is of high value
for catalytic reactions such as steam reforming of methane towards hy-
drogen production [157] and catalytic decomposition of methane
(CDM) [168]. Titration of the surface with acid/basic molecules (e.g.
CO2 and NH3) followed by Temperature Programmed Desorption
(TPD) is a mainstream method for evaluating the overall surface basic
and acidic sites [169]. In order to differentiate between Lewis and
Brönsted-Lowry adsorption of volatile amines (e.g. pyridine) and/or cat-
alytic probe reactions that are exclusively favored over Lewis or
Brönsted-Lowry type of centers have been successfully proposed.

6.2. Thermal catalysis
Catalysis is known for its essential role in the chemical industry par-
ticularly in facilitating the production of added-value hydrocarbons,
chemicals and fuels (e.g. CH4, H2, CH3OH) [30]. Besides, catalysts are
being studied to be employed for the removal of fuel impurities, such
as sulfur, towards producing cleaner fuels with desirable properties
(e.g. ignition, viscosity, etc.) [175]. There is a major challenge when it
comes to efﬁcient metal catalysts design, mainly in overcoming the co-
hesive energy that drives co-domain formation [172] and sintering at el-
evated temperatures [176]. Therefore, there is an immense effort
tailoring catalytic systems that stabilize noble metal single atoms and
nanoparticles at high temperature. In this section, high entropy oxides
are being discussed in the catalysis ﬁeld where they have shown their
superiority in thermal/photo and electro-catalysis for a versatile spec-
trum of reaction spanning from oxidation to reduction.

PtNiMgCuCoZn)Ox, an entropy stabilized oxide, which had rock-salt sin-
gle phase, by the method of Pt co-deposition. In the synthesis procedure,
the transition metals precursors were dissolved in distilled water and
stirred, then the noble metal precursor was added to the prepared solu-
tion (one pot synthesis). The material exhibited high dispersion of Pt
which conﬁrmed that the six (6) cations crystalized homogenously to

Zhang et al. [126] tested the ability of combining high entropy oxides

6.5. CO2 hydrogenation
Reducing CO2 to CO by H2 was also investigated using Noble
Metals-HEOs. On the contrary to the used HEO catalysts, Chen et al.
[53] prepared (2–5 wt%) Ru/Pt-(NiMgCuZnCo)O catalyst under ambient
conditions, and showed the effect of the gained maximized entropy on
the loading and dispersion of the noble metals. Although 5wt%(Heat
Treated at 500 °C) Pt-(NiMgCuZnCo)O was the only catalyst that could
reduce up to 47.8% of CO2 , but all the synthesized systems exhibited
high selectivity to CO 95%, and high catalytic stability that could reach
up to 12 hours at 500 °C without any signiﬁcant fall backs in their per-
formance. This result was justiﬁed using HAADF-STEM characterization
that showed neither aggregations nor segregations of the loaded Pt/Ru
in (NiMgCuZnCo)O, and the HEOs’ latent properties that enable them
to perform actively at harsh temperature conditions, and behave as a
stabilizing support which disperse the active noble metals as
nanoclusters (2–3 nm) and single atoms. Towards CO as a main product,
the reported systems can be further employed to be active for the re-
verse water gas shift reaction, or the systems compositions can be
tuned and be exploited to convert CO2 to CH4.

with MoNiCuZnCoOx composition using ball milling followed by heat
treating at T ≥900 °C. The applied process eventually led to the forma-

6.8. CH4 partial oxidation
A technical report that was published by Stanford University for
Global Climate and Energy project [137] demonstrated the utilization of
HEO for CH4 partial oxidation. It showed that (MgNiZnCuCo)Ox,
(MgTiZnCuCo)Ox (AlFeCrGaTi)Ox, (FeCoMgNiMn)Ox, (MgCaNiCoFeMn)
Ox and Cu nanoparticles supported by the previous catalysts resulted in
oxidizing CH4 partially to CO2 and CO in temperatures that ranged be-
tween 25–500 °C. The research was targeting producing methanol out
of CH4, but it concluded that the used elements played an important
role in determining the route of the reaction where the excessive amount
of active O in the tested HEOs redirected the selectivity from methanol to
carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide. Besides, the metal-oxygen bond-
ing, and the condition of the high temperature was able only to activate
the methane molecule without any signiﬁcant effect on producing the de-
sired reaction yield.

2 h only. The reported catalyst showed superior performance in terms
of stability and activity compared to Au/Pd-TiO2 that achieved a maxi-

period. The prominent catalytic activity was explained by the existence of

7. Computational studies
This section presents the computational studies that have been
employed so far to unravel the properties which are directly related to
the structure and catalytic performance of the high entropy oxides.
Modelling high entropy oxides unit cells with taking into consideration
all the possible conﬁgurations with the intrinsic oxygen vacancies is still
not reported in literature; though such a study is expected to enlighten
the HEO potential as catalysts. To the best of our knowledge, over the
last ﬁve years, few computational studies of high entropy oxides were
published in the ﬁeld, highlighting the difﬁculty dealing with such com-
plex structures. As explained previously, the population and mobility of
the oxygen vacancies play a crucial role in improving the performance
of the catalysts. Density Functional Theory (DFT) is a powerful tool
used to validate the experimental results by studying the low (e.g.
CeO2 and CeSmCuO) and medium entropy (e.g. BaSrCoFeO) [193] ox-
ides at their atomistic level and calculate the energy of formation of ox-
ygen vacancies, located at the most preferable sites, and their behavior
under strain. Up to date publications show a clear gap in describing
high entropy oxides’ oxygen vacancies quantitatively by DFT. In what
follows, the use of computational tools on difference aspects of HEO is
discussed; from the point of formation (mixing) to intrinsic properties
(e.g. band gap).
7.1. HEO mixing behavior
A case study published by Zhong et al. [194] where a new computa-
tional approach applied by CALculation of PHAse Diagram (CALPHAD)
was adapted to evaluate the difference between the mixing in the
high entropy ceramics and the high entropy alloys on the simple perov-
skite model (LaMnO3±δ). The study deﬁned clearly that mixing in
HEOs stands for the disorder of the cations, anions and defects sites
while in the HEAs it only refers to the disorder of the main constituents
that contributes with more than 5 mol% in the material. The study
highlighted the difﬁculty of recording the mixing in high entropy ce-
ramics in general due to the different contribution of the lattice constit-
uents (cations and anions) and the lattice defect chemistry.
7.2. Phase stability
From a thermodynamic point a view, Anand et al. [26] conducted a
computational study and investigated the effect of decreasing/changing
the number of components/ composition of HEO (NiMgCoCuZn)O on
the enthalpy and conﬁgurational entropy through optimized lattice
conﬁgurations of cations and anions. In terms of the compositions,
(CoCuMgNi)O and (CoMgNiZn)O achieved the lowest mixing en-
thalpies. And, in terms of the number of components, it was observed
that adding Ca to the system as in (CaCoCuNiZn)O increased the mixing
enthalpy which indicated its favorability to form secondary phases
[195]. It was proved that the HEO system is entropy stabilized since
the enthalpy contribution was positive and signiﬁcantly low. Decreasing
the number of cations from 5 to 4 affects directly on lowering the
mixing entropy and increasing the enthalpy, which requires greater
temperature compensation to stabilize the phase of the system and
drive it to lower free energy. Changing the composition to include Ca
among the cations instead of

7.6. Energy of formation
Nguyen et al. [134] simulated using DFT (VASP) three supercells of
three different high entropy oxides with the following constituents for
the rock-salt systems: Mg, Zn, Mn, Co, and Ni and Cr, Fe, Mn, Co and
Ni for the spinel system. The study investigated the effect of involving
non-equiatomic elements in the HEO, therefore, the two rock-salt sys-
tems varied in terms of the compositional concentration, similarly for
the spinel system. The geometries were relaxed and the energy of for-
mation was calculated through comparing the total energy of the
HEOs and their corresponding compositional elements at their ground
state. DFT is a powerful computational tool to unravel the favorability
of a structure formation as the study employed it to augment the syn-
chrotron XRD results as the modelled HEOs tended to crystalize in
rock-salt structure in the regions where Mn concentration is low and
Zn or Ni concentration is high. On the contrary, the spinel structure is
more stable when the Mn concentration is high.
8. Current challenges and future work
Selecting the components to synthesize new families of high entropy
oxides and tune it so to serve the targeted catalysis chemistry is consid-
ered to be a challenge. Elements are chosen for their diversity in struc-
tures, coordination and cationic radii to test entropic contribution and
at the same time they should act as active sites for the intended reaction.
One of the main challenges that encounters the novel class of materials
is the fact that many still unexplored parameters contribute along with
conﬁgurational entropy to drive the system to the single phase, espe-
cially in the rare-earth based systems. As Djenadic et al. [48] reported,
that thermal treatments of systems that did not contain Ce did not crys-
tallize into a single phase unlike the Ce containing ones. Indeed, this in-
dicates that the still unraveled reason that enables Ce to homogenize
multiphase systems is conjugated to the possessed high entropy of the
systems. Does this apply to other rare-earth elements, such as La, Sm,
Pr and Gd? Which ones of the rare-earth elements offer a stabilization
factor? This is a question worth-addressing. Also, what is the balance
between the enthalpy penalty during the mixing and the stabilizing en-
tropy for the different elements among the rare earth and transition
metals? Due to the numerous applications of those elements in cataly-
sis, the above questions are worthy to be addressed.
The study by Djenadic et al. [48] expanded the exploration to reach
up to 7-components per system (CeGdLaNdPrSmY)O, where a clear for-
mation of a minor secondary phase of La2O3 in the bixbyite Ia-3 crystal
structure was noticed. Those secondary phases are formed due to ‘slug-
gish’ diffusion phenomena and it can be explained by that high number
of elements are participating equally in the crystal and therefore they
are competing for the same position in the lattice. Thus, more time is
needed for the all the elements to reach their dedicated random sites.
Overcoming this obstacle can be achieved by increasing the retention
time of the components during synthesis or by subjecting it to a thermal
treatment. System with 3-6 elements that contained Ce form a single-
phase regardless of the thermal treatment and based on this study, en-
tropy effect is not in charge of the phase stabilization in REOs. Another
worth studying question would be how the secondary phases can be

Analytical Tools: Characterization of HEOs covered the conventional
techniques (e.g. XRD, SEM, EDS, HR-TEM, BET surface area, Raman and
XPS, isotopic studies) to explore their main properties. Engaging more
techniques, such as H2-TPR and CO2 and NH3-TPD for determining the
redox properties as well as the basicity/acidity of the sites are also still
not reported in literature, whereas isotopic studies would deﬁnitely
enlighten the oxygen mobility dynamics in the complex HEO lattice.
HEOs have been utilized for thermal catalysis, photocatalysis and
electrocatalysis with a relatively unpreceded performance. The mate-
rials still need to be understood using more advanced spectroscopies
(e.g. EXAFS) so to further capture all those treats that trigger their com-
plex structure towards a remarkable catalytic activity.
9.2. Reported catalytic applications
High entropy oxides showed a high activity in several catalytic reac-
tions across the ﬁeld of thermal, photo- and electro-catalysis, such as:
CO oxidation, alcohol oxidation, CH4 partial oxidation, oxidative desul-
furization and CO2 hydrogenation, water oxidation and water splitting
(H2 evolution). The CO oxidation is the most well-studied catalytic reac-
tion so far. Getting insight information for other reactions such as those
mentioned above is still missing in the literature.
9.3. Implications for design
Both transition metal (TM) oxides and rare earth (RE) oxides were
used to synthesize entropy driven oxide catalysts. Among the impor-
tant, but not the only ones, design parameters, are the conﬁgurational
entropy and the enthalpy of mixing. Other crucial factors dictating the
synthesizability of particular HEO systems are the valence and the coor-
dination environment compatibility as veriﬁed by experimental studies
as well as ab initio calculations.
