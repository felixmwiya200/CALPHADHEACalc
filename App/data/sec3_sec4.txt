of the counterpart oxides. As discussed by Rost et al. [7], considering

to HEA system due to the existence of arranged arrays of oxygen atoms.

3.2. Entropy stabilized oxides (ESO) vs. high entropy oxides (HEO)
Also, it is important to draw the attention that even though “high-
entropy and entropy-stabilization are often used interchangeably, they
are fundamentally different” [17]. The concept of high entropy reﬂects
that the system simply has high conﬁgurational entropy without any
consideration to the endothermicity or exothermicity of the system
[7,17,19]. However, entropy stabilization indicates that the system
needs to possess both high conﬁgurational entropy and positive en-
thalpy of formation (+ΔH) which means that the entropy drives the
thermodynamic state of stability [17]. It should also be noted that
many studies reported did not verify the entropic stabilization of their
materials since the reversibility tests and the Differential Scanning Cal-
orimetry (DSC) characterization were not discussed [45,56]. Overall,
conﬁgurational disorder gives a way to discover new different phases
of crystalline matter and ﬁnd new opportunities for property
engineering.
3.3. Reversibility
Based on Rost et al. [7] study, reversibility of the ESO was deﬁned by
subjecting the material’s pellets to calcination in air (~ 700–1100 °C)
and following it by quenching at room temperature. The sample was
characterized by X-ray diffraction as shown in Fig. 6 where the detected
peaks demonstrated the existence of minor phases at lower tempera-
tures of the heat treatment. Multi-phases of rocksalt and tenorite were
observed after 700 °C, as the tenorite peaks intensity decreased with
the signiﬁcant increase of temperature to crystalize into rocksalt single
phase between 850 and 900 °C. Since reversibility is a requirement for
entropy driven transitions, low temperature should transform the pel-
lets back to multi-phase state, as demonstrated in Fig. 4.
3.4. High vs. medium vs. low entropy oxides
In Section 2, Fig. 3 shows the three classiﬁcations of the metal-oxide
systems as Cantor et al. [11] and Yeh at al. [12] had established. Low en-
tropy oxides are deﬁned to be composed out of less than 3 different
cations, where their conﬁgurational entropy values are less than 1 R
[41]. Similarly, for the medium entropy oxides systems, the conﬁgura-
tional disorder values usually range between 1 R up to 1.5 R which indi-
cates that they consist of 3 to 4 different cations [10,41]. While the high
entropy oxides are distinguished by being composed from 5 or more
different cations and as a result, their mixing entropy will be greater
than 1.5 R. (R=8.314 J/K.mol) [10]. Rost et al. [7] and Djenadic et al.
[48] studied the effect of changing the composition and the number of
components in the HEO system from two different perspectives which
were the change in entropy that affected the phases and the structure,
respectively. Starting by Rost et al. [7] composition test on the MgO,
CoO, NiO, CuO and ZnO system is conducted to further check the
phase transition. If the entropy is the driving force then changing the
cation ratios will alter the transition temperature, and equi-molarity
will lead to the lowest transformation temperature. When there is a de-
viation from equi-molarity, number of the conﬁgurational microstates
(Ω) are reduced according to Sconf = kB log (Ω) and this will therefore
increase the transition temperature as Sconf(xi) is linked logarithmically
to the mole fraction. The study showed that conﬁgurational entropy for
the solution that has N species and each individual species was alerted
with keeping the rest equimolar. A couple of dependencies were ob-
served, entropy increase as species are added and it was the maximum
when all of them have the same fraction. In both cases, ideal random
mixing is assumed. Composition-varying experiments were preformed
to observe the single phase rocksalt formation trends where all the
sets are equilibrated at 875 °C for 12 hours and removing any compo-
nent ends with distorting the lattice by minor multi-phases.
4. High entropy oxides synthesis methods
4.1. HEO selection rules
Rost et al. [7], Chen et al. [44] and Djenadic et al. [48] reported the de-
sign rules of their HEO systems where the cations have an overarching
role on the properties and behavior of the materials. Therefore, the dif-
ference of the cationic radii of the components should be relatively
small in order to have a single-phase oxide. Besides, at least one of the
cations’ crystal structure should be different and the overall system
should have distinctive electronegativity. Intensive solubility of the
components was also considered to be a selection rule. Also, as a mini-
mum, one binary oxide system should not have a complete miscibility
gap at 0.5 mole fraction. In summary, the geometric and electrical bal-
ance factors [27] are critical factors for determining the constituents of
the HEO systems where the uniformity of crystal structures, electro-
negativities and coordination numbers is avoided while designing.

Summarizing the main ﬁndings from the previous references, the
following remarks can be done:
Sono- and mechano-chemical methods succeeded in stabilizing the
entropy without the need of the high temperature as in the solid state
synthesis with metal salts precursors. The structures of the HEO re-
ported so far are rock-salt, ﬂuorite, spinel, perovskites, aurivillius,

The harsh solid-state synthesis conditions enable the materials to
gain outstanding properties such as: withstanding high temperature
in the different applications compared to the conventional oxides. In
other words, high entropy oxides are usually formed after being sub-
jected to high temperatures that could reach up to 900 °C. Therefore,
they can be the perfect candidates for excessive temperature applica-
tions without losing their main properties.
Morphology of different HEO materials varied with changing the
synthesis method and the composition. As shown in Fig. 7(A–C), the
same composition of the transition metal oxides (CoCuMgNiZn)O was
synthesized using NSP, FSP and reverse co-precipitation (RCP) [17].
The study had reported important observations on the SEM micro-
graphs, the morphology was varying in size and distribution, where
the shape was approximately uniﬁed for the different methods (spher-
ical). RCP method yielded in having spherical agglomerates, with a con-
siderably greater diameter (~50–150 nm) compared to FSP and NSP.
NSP particles exhibited a wide spectrum of sizes (nano-microparticles)
and a variation between spheres and cuboidals. Fig. 7(E) depicts the
same composition of HEO which was synthesized by the same method
(NSP), the morphology also varies between the cuboidal and the spher-
ical with a broad size distribution [111]. In the Fig. 7(D), the
(CeZrYLaGd)O material was synthesized by hydrothermal methods
and clearly exhibited a homogeneous distribution of particles with the
creation of pores due to the selected components [125]. SEM image
over the (CoCuFeMnNi)O is shown in Fig. 7(F), and clearly its morphol-
ogy deviates compared to the one in Fig. 7(C) although they share the
same synthesis method (RCP) due to the change in the composition
(addition of Fe and Mn instead of Mg and Zn) [104]. Fig. 7(J) depicts a
clear Ce decomposition and the possible reasons were the following:
the miscibility gap between the liquid and solid phase that was formed
upon sintering at high temperatures, or the composed solid solution is
deviating from ideality and considered to be sub-regular, where the
Gibbs free energy curve is asymmetric [17]. Having the material devi-
ant from the homogeneous phase (phase impurities) can cause draw-
backs on the catalytic performance by deactivating the catalyst under
stream. Fig. 7(G–I) and (K–M) demonstrate the different morphol-
ogies that HEO systems can possess through reactive spark plasma
sintering, precipitation, solid state and the oxidation of high entropy
alloys as precursors and should be studied thoroughly to explain the
role of the particles size and distribution in the catalytic applications
[74,82,104,123,129,150].
A direct link between the synthesis methods and the textural prop-
erties of the catalysts was noticed. For instance, ball milling of
NiCoMgZnCuO achieved a BET surface area of 2 m2/g [44], while
coprecipitation (using precipitating agent NaOH and CTAB) of the
same system of transition metals recorded higher BET surface area
(28 m2/g) [44]. Another observation should be raised, generally high en-
tropy oxides basic preparation (solid state) methods depend on high
temperature to crystallize the material that also tend to limit the size
of the powder particles (sintering cannot be avoided) and prevent it
from forming nanoparticles [22]. However, NSP and FSP methods are
suggested semi-effective solutions to reach the nano-scale particles
due to the short retention time of the material [22]. But, it should be
noted that agglomeration of particles is also inevitable which can
again limit the NSP and FSP techniques [22].
